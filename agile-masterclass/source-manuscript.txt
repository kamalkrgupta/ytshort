Episode 1 | Agile Testing Fundamentals
Professional instructor narration • English • Target: approximately 10 minutes
00:00–01:00  |  Hook and introduction
Imagine your team has spent three months developing a mobile application. The design looks perfect, the developers are confident, and the client is excited. But one day before launch, your testing team discovers that users cannot complete their payments. The launch gets delayed, development costs increase, and customer confidence suffers. Now imagine discovering the same issue during the first week of development. That is exactly where Agile Testing makes a difference. Welcome to this lesson. We will explain what Agile Testing is, how it differs from traditional testing, and how software teams apply it in practice.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Show a launch countdown, failed payment screen and a team identifying the issue early.
01:00–02:15  |  What is Agile Testing?
Agile Testing is an approach in which testing happens continuously throughout software development. Rather than waiting for developers to finish an entire application, testers collaborate with them from the beginning. Consider an e-commerce website. In the first sprint, developers implement login while testers prepare scenarios for valid credentials, incorrect passwords, empty fields and security. In the next sprint, the team builds a shopping cart, and testers verify both the new feature and existing login behavior. This process continues throughout delivery. The key idea is to identify problems early, resolve them quickly, and continuously improve the product.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Animated cycle: Plan → Develop → Test → Feedback → Plan.
02:15–03:30  |  Agile versus traditional testing
In a typical Waterfall project, work moves through sequential phases: requirements, design, implementation and testing. Major defects may remain undiscovered until late in delivery. Agile works differently. Development and testing take place together in short iterations, commonly called sprints. Testers collaborate with developers, Product Owners and stakeholders throughout the process. Frequent feedback allows teams to adjust priorities. If a customer requests a discount feature, the team can clarify it, prioritize it and validate it in an upcoming sprint. The objective is not merely to work faster but to validate that the team is building the right product at the right quality level.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Side-by-side Waterfall sequence and iterative Agile feedback loops.
03:30–04:45  |  Five core principles
First, continuous testing means that quality is evaluated throughout development. Second, shared responsibility means developers, testers and other team members contribute to quality. Third, early feedback enables problems to be investigated before additional work depends on them. Fourth, customer collaboration ensures regular feedback on real business needs. Fifth, working software matters: a feature is not complete merely because coding has finished. It should satisfy agreed acceptance criteria and the Definition of Done. Together, these principles encourage teams to build quality into the product instead of treating testing as a final inspection.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Five animated principle cards with one practical example each.
04:45–06:00  |  Agile Testing in Scrum
Consider a two-week Scrum sprint. During Sprint Planning, the team selects Product Backlog Items supporting a Sprint Goal. A user story might say: As a customer, I want to reset my password so that I can regain access to my account. Before development, the team clarifies acceptance criteria. What happens if the address is unregistered? When does the reset link expire? What happens if the link is reused? During the sprint, developers implement the functionality while testers prepare and execute checks. The team resolves defects collaboratively. At the Sprint Review, stakeholders inspect the increment and provide feedback. At the Retrospective, the team identifies improvements to its process.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Sprint board: Backlog → In Progress → Testing → Done; add review and retrospective.
06:00–07:15  |  Four Agile Testing quadrants
The four Agile Testing quadrants help teams choose complementary kinds of testing. Quadrant one focuses on technology-facing tests supporting development, such as unit and component tests. Quadrant two includes business-facing examples and acceptance tests that guide development. Quadrant three evaluates the product from a user and business perspective, including exploratory and usability testing. Quadrant four examines technical qualities such as performance, security and reliability. In a banking application, a unit test can check interest calculations, an acceptance test can check money transfers, exploratory testing can reveal confusing interactions, and load testing can examine heavy usage. The quadrants are not sequential phases.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Display a labelled two-by-two quadrant matrix with concrete examples.
07:15–08:30  |  Real-world example
Imagine a food delivery application. Sprint one delivers registration and login. The tester checks valid and invalid credentials, recovery and security scenarios. Sprint two introduces restaurant listings and search; testers examine accuracy, filters and empty results. Sprint three adds the shopping cart and payment integration. The team tests payment success, payment failure, duplicate transactions and order confirmation. Importantly, it also verifies that previously completed functionality still works. This is regression testing. Continuous testing across sprints reduces the chance that major problems are discovered only before release.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Three sprint timeline with login, search, and cart/payment; show regression checks across sprints.
08:30–09:20  |  Tools and automation
Tools can support fast feedback. Jira can track user stories, bugs and sprint progress. Postman can validate APIs. Selenium and Playwright can automate browser testing. Appium supports mobile automation, and Jenkins or GitHub Actions can run tests in continuous integration pipelines. Automation is useful for repetitive checks, but it does not replace human investigation. Exploratory testing still depends on judgment and curiosity. Choose tools based on risks, technology and testing needs. The goal is not to automate everything; it is to obtain useful, reliable feedback efficiently.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Tool categories and a simple CI pipeline; avoid implying that any tool guarantees quality.
09:20–10:00  |  Recap and outro
Agile Testing is continuous and collaborative. Testing begins early and continues throughout development. Scrum creates regular opportunities for planning, inspection and improvement. The testing quadrants help balance business and technical quality, while automation makes repetitive checks more efficient. The most important takeaway is that quality is not added at the end; it is built into every stage of delivery. In the next episode, we will compare Agile methodologies in greater detail. Knowledge check: why can identifying a defect during the first sprint be less costly than discovering it just before launch?

Episode 2 | Agile Testing Methodologies
Professional instructor narration • English • Target: approximately 10 minutes
00:00–01:00  |  Introduction
Agile is a set of values and principles rather than a single prescribed process. In this lesson, we examine Scrum, Kanban, Extreme Programming and Lean Software Development, and explain how testing changes with each approach.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Four methodology cards with short definitions.
01:00–03:00  |  Scrum
Scrum organizes complex work into sprints, commonly one to four weeks. Consider a customer management portal. During planning, the team selects a registration story. The Product Owner clarifies acceptance criteria, including valid addresses, invalid addresses and duplicate registration. Developers and testers collaborate during the sprint, and stakeholders inspect the increment at the Sprint Review. The Retrospective identifies ways to improve. Testing is integrated throughout the sprint rather than postponed to its final days.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Animated two-week sprint timeline.
03:00–04:45  |  Kanban
Kanban visualizes work and limits work in progress. A maintenance team may use Backlog, Ready, Development, Testing and Done columns. If Testing has a WIP limit of three and already contains three items, the team focuses on completing existing work rather than starting more. This makes bottlenecks visible and supports steady delivery, especially for teams receiving unpredictable support requests.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Kanban board with a three-item testing WIP limit.
04:45–06:30  |  Extreme Programming
Extreme Programming, or XP, emphasizes engineering practices including test-driven development, pair programming and continuous integration. In TDD, a developer writes a failing test, writes enough code to pass it and then refactors safely. This Red–Green–Refactor cycle works well for calculations and business rules. Testing becomes part of writing the software rather than a separate final checkpoint.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Red–Green–Refactor animation and unit-test example.
06:30–08:00  |  Lean Software Development
Lean focuses on customer value and reducing waste. Waste in testing might include waiting for test environments, duplicate paperwork or slow feedback from an oversized test suite. Lean does not mean skipping important tests. A team might run fast smoke tests on each commit and more comprehensive regression tests later, preserving coverage while reducing feedback time.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Before-and-after CI feedback timeline.
08:00–09:30  |  Choosing an approach
A feature-development team may benefit from Scrum; a support operation may benefit from Kanban; a calculation-intensive product may benefit from XP practices. Teams can combine approaches. The right selection depends on delivery constraints, technical risk and team maturity.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Three project cards and comparative matrix.
09:30–10:00  |  Recap
Scrum emphasizes iterative inspection, Kanban focuses on flow, XP strengthens engineering discipline, and Lean reduces waste. Knowledge check: which approach would help a support team receiving unpredictable requests, and why?
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Four-key-takeaway recap slide.

Episode 3 | Role of a Tester in Agile
Professional instructor narration • English • Target: approximately 10 minutes
00:00–01:00  |  Introduction
An Agile tester does more than discover bugs. Testing expertise contributes to requirement analysis, risk identification, test design, collaboration, automation and improvement. Follow a tester working on an online banking application.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Tester collaborating with a cross-functional team.
01:00–02:30  |  Requirements refinement
For a money-transfer story, ask: What if funds are insufficient? Can the transfer amount be negative? What is the maximum limit? What happens after a timeout? Can the same transaction be processed twice? These questions turn a vague requirement into testable acceptance criteria.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Banking user story with five clarifying questions.
02:30–04:00  |  Developer collaboration
As developers build a transfer API, testers review its contract and prepare positive, negative and boundary scenarios. They can verify the API before the interface exists. This early feedback prevents misunderstandings from becoming expensive defects.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  API contract and three scenario types.
04:00–05:30  |  Test execution
Functional testing checks expected behavior, integration testing checks component interactions, regression testing checks previously working behavior and exploratory testing investigates surprises. For a bank transfer, investigate retries after a network timeout and document expected versus actual behavior.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Sample defect report with redacted transaction data.
05:30–07:00  |  Communication
In daily collaboration, describe progress and blockers clearly: yesterday I validated the transfer API; today I am testing failure recovery; the payment sandbox is unavailable. The tester collaborates with developers, product stakeholders and operations to remove obstacles.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Daily team update with progress and blocker.
07:00–08:30  |  Technical skills
Understanding SQL, APIs, version control, browser automation and CI/CD helps testers verify behavior independently. Postman can inspect responses, SQL can check persisted records, and Playwright can automate critical web journeys. Not every tester needs to specialize in every tool.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Postman, database and browser-testing workflow.
08:30–09:30  |  Effectiveness
Bug counts alone are a weak measure. Preventing defects during refinement may be more valuable than reporting avoidable defects late. Consider feedback speed, important risk coverage, escaped defects and reliability of checks.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Balanced testing metrics dashboard.
09:30–10:00  |  Recap
Agile testers combine analysis, communication and technical judgment. Their goal is to help the whole team prevent defects and deliver reliable software. Knowledge check: name three questions to ask before building money transfers.
VISUAL / ON-SCREEN DIRECTION  Three learning takeaways.
